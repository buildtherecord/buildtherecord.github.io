The instinct to tidy up is strong and it is a trap.

Once you are in a dispute — or once you can reasonably see one coming — **stop
deleting things.** Not the blurry photos. Not the voicemail. Not the text thread
where you were angry. Not the app that has your service history in it.

## Why this is more serious than it feels

Courts and reviewers take the destruction of relevant records **very** seriously,
and the reaction is often out of proportion to what was destroyed. The reasoning
is simple: if you got rid of it, the assumption available to everyone is that it
would have hurt you.

That assumption can end up costing more than the actual content of whatever you
deleted. **A bad fact you kept is survivable. A missing thing you deleted is
sometimes not.**

You do not need to know the legal term for this. You need the habit: once a
dispute exists, nothing goes away.

## What people delete without thinking

- Photos that "didn't come out" — a blurry photo still carries a date
- Texts, once the thread gets uncomfortable
- The angry email draft they never sent
- Voicemails, after listening
- App accounts and portals — closing the account can take the records with it
- Old phones, traded in with the only copy of something on them
- Emails auto-deleted by a 30-day rule you set up years ago and forgot

**Check that last one today.** Automatic deletion is still deletion, and "my email
did it on its own" is a bad position to be in.

## What to do instead

**Turn off auto-delete** in mail, messages, and anything else that prunes itself.

**Back up the whole thing, once, now.** Not the good bits — everything. Phone to
computer, computer to a second place. Do not curate. Curating later is easy;
recovering later is not.

**Keep originals separate from working copies.** One folder you never touch, one
folder you work in. If you crop or annotate, do it to a copy.

**Download from portals before anything closes.** Insurer portals, service
records, contractor apps. Those disappear when accounts lapse, and they are not
yours to get back.

**Do not "clean up" the file before showing a lawyer.** They would much rather see
everything, including the parts you are embarrassed by. Removing them is the one
thing that can genuinely damage your position, and lawyers are unbothered by
messy — they are bothered by gaps.

## The angry draft

Keep it. Do not send it, but keep it.

If you are worried about it being read one day: the fact that you wrote something
angry and *did not send it* is not the problem you think it is. Deleting it, in a
dispute, is a worse fact than having written it.

## The one-line version

**Tidy later. Right now, keep everything, back it up once, and turn off anything
that deletes on its own.**

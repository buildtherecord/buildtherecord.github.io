Do you need a lawyer?

Some honest signals, and how to find one — including for free.

This project cannot tell you whether you need a lawyer, and cannot tell you anything about your particular situation. What it can do is lay out the signals that usually mean get help, and show you where to look.

The record you are building is useful either way. If you end up with a lawyer, it is the thing that lets them start fast instead of billing you to sort through a shoebox.

Signals that usually mean talk to someone

Anything with a deadline you might already have missed. This is the most urgent one on the list. Do not wait to be organized first.

You have been sued, or served with anything. Court papers have short clocks and hard consequences.

The amount is large relative to your life. Not a fixed number — large for you.

Someone has raised the possibility of you being at fault, of a countersuit, or of anything criminal.

There is a contract you signed and do not understand, especially with arbitration or "you agree to waive" language.

Your health, housing, immigration status, or custody is involved. These are categories where mistakes are hard to undo.

You have been asked to sign a release or settlement. Once you sign, it is usually over. That is the moment to get a second opinion, not after.

Signals it may be reasonable to keep going yourself

The amount is small and bounded. There is a defined process built for non-lawyers — an internal appeal, a small-claims court, a licensing board complaint. Nobody is accusing you of anything. And there is no deadline you cannot comfortably meet.

Plenty of disputes are resolved by an organized person with a good paper record and no lawyer at all. That is what this method is for.

Where to look — free and low-cost first

Your state bar's lawyer referral service. Nearly every state has one. Often a short consultation for a reduced fee, sometimes free.

[lawhelp.org](https://www.lawhelp.org) — free and low-cost legal help, sorted by state and by problem type.

[ABA Free Legal Answers](https://abafreelegalanswers.org) — post a civil legal question and a volunteer attorney in your state answers it, free, if you meet the income guidelines.

Legal aid in your county. Usually income-limited, and worth calling even if you think you earn too much — the limits are often higher than people assume, and they can refer you onward.

Law school clinics. Many take real cases, supervised by faculty, at no charge.

Bar association pro bono programs, and for employment, housing or benefits matters, the specialist nonprofits in that area.

What to bring

Your record. Dated, sourced, organized, with the weak parts flagged.

A first meeting spent watching someone read your timeline is worth several spent watching them read your emails. Bring a one-page summary, the timeline, and the documents in order — and tell them plainly what you have not been able to prove.

Two things worth knowing

A consultation is not a commitment. Many are free or cheap, and "should I be worried about this?" is a perfectly good reason to book one.

Some cases cost you nothing up front. In areas like consumer protection, lemon law, and some employment matters, lawyers may work on contingency, or the law may require the other side to pay your legal fees if you win. It is worth asking directly: "Do you take these on contingency, and are fees recoverable here?" The answer is sometimes yes, and people who assume otherwise never ask.

Not legal advice This is practical guidance on collecting and keeping evidence, not advice about your case. Rules vary by state. When it matters, bring what you have to a lawyer — your state bar runs a referral service, and lawhelp.org lists free and low-cost help.
